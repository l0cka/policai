The [Online Safety (Basic Online Safety Expectations) Determination 2022](/policies/federal-online-safety-basic-online-safety-expectations-determination-2022) sets out what the Australian Government expects of social media services, relevant electronic services and designated internet services ([Federal Register of Legislation, F2022L00062](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/asmade/details)). The Online Safety (Basic Online Safety Expectations) Amendment Determination 2024 inserted section 8A, which deals with generative artificial intelligence, along with section 8B on recommender systems ([F2024L00590 as made](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00590/asmade/2024-05-30/text/original/pdf); [compilation endnotes](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/2024-05-31/2024-05-31/text/original/pdf)). The current compilation of the Determination took effect on 31 May 2024 ([compilation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/2024-05-31/2024-05-31/text/original/pdf)).

## What it says

Section 8A applies "if the service uses or enables the use of generative artificial intelligence capabilities". It sets two expectations ([s 8A(1)–(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/2024-05-31/2024-05-31/text/original/pdf)):

- **Safety by design:** the provider "will take reasonable steps to consider end-user safety and incorporate safety measures in the design, implementation and maintenance of generative artificial intelligence capabilities on the service".
- **Harmful output:** the provider "will take reasonable steps to proactively minimise the extent to which generative artificial intelligence capabilities may be used to produce material or facilitate activity that is unlawful or harmful".

The Determination gives four examples of reasonable steps, without limiting either expectation ([s 8A(3)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/2024-05-31/2024-05-31/text/original/pdf)):

- assessing safety risks and impacts, mitigating identified risks, and running safety review processes "throughout the design, development, deployment and post-deployment stages";
- giving end-users educational or explanatory tools about the service's generative AI capabilities and their risks, including when new features are integrated;
- "ensuring, to the extent reasonably practicable, that training material for generative artificial intelligence capabilities and models do not contain unlawful or harmful material";
- "ensuring, to the extent reasonably practicable, that generative artificial intelligence capabilities can detect and prevent the execution of prompts that generate unlawful or harmful material".

The same amendment added an expectation that a provider will take reasonable steps to make the best interests of the child "a primary consideration in the design and operation of any service that is likely to be accessed by children" ([s 6(2A)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/2024-05-31/2024-05-31/text/original/pdf)).

The expectations are not directly enforceable. The Online Safety Act 2021 says a determination of basic online safety expectations "does not impose a duty that is enforceable by proceedings in a court" ([s 45(4)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2021A00076/2026-09-12/2026-09-12/text/original/pdf)). The eSafety Commissioner can instead:

- require a provider, by written notice, to report on how it is meeting the expectations, with a civil penalty of 500 penalty units for failing to comply with the notice ([ss 49–50](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2021A00076/2026-09-12/2026-09-12/text/original/pdf));
- prepare, give to the provider and, where appropriate, publish a statement that the provider has contravened, or has complied with, the expectations ([s 48](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2021A00076/2026-09-12/2026-09-12/text/original/pdf)).

## Who it affects

The expectations apply to providers of social media services, relevant electronic services and designated internet services ([record](/policies/federal-online-safety-basic-online-safety-expectations-determination-2022); [Online Safety Act s 45](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2021A00076/2026-09-12/2026-09-12/text/original/pdf)). Section 8A applies to any of those services that uses generative AI capabilities or enables their use ([s 8A](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00062/2024-05-31/2024-05-31/text/original/pdf)). The register lists the Department of Infrastructure, Transport, Regional Development, Communications, Sport and the Arts and the eSafety Commissioner as the responsible agencies ([record](/policies/federal-online-safety-basic-online-safety-expectations-determination-2022)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, section 8A works through transparency rather than direct obligation. A provider cannot be taken to court for falling short of section 8A. The pressure comes from reporting notices, which are enforceable, and from published statements of non-compliance.

In Policai's analysis, the examples in section 8A(3) read as a lifecycle checklist: risk assessment before and after release, user education, cleaner training data and prompt filtering. The phrase "to the extent reasonably practicable" on the training-data and prompt examples leaves room for providers to argue about what is feasible at scale.

In Policai's analysis, section 8A now sits beside the enforceable [eSafety industry codes](/policies/esafety-genai-industry-codes), which cover generative AI services and AI companion chatbots and have applied since 9 March 2026 ([record](/policies/esafety-genai-industry-codes)). The register records that the Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026 would repeal those codes and standards 12 months after Royal Assent ([record](/policies/esafety-genai-industry-codes)); Policai's [note on the bill](/analysis/digital-duty-of-care-bill-2026) covers that change. This note does not assess whether the bill changes Part 4 of the Act or the expectations in this Determination.
