Who regulates artificial intelligence in Australia, and through which
instruments? There is no single answer, because there is no general AI law.
AI is governed in five layers: laws that apply to AI as they apply to any
technology, a small number of laws written for AI, policies that govern how
each government uses AI, voluntary national guidance, and the rules courts
and tribunals set for the people who appear before them. This guide explains
each layer and links to the register records behind it.

## Is there an Australian AI Act?

No. In September 2024 the Department of Industry, Science and Resources
released a
[proposals paper on mandatory guardrails for AI in high-risk settings](/policies/safe-and-responsible-ai-australia-proposals-paper),
which sought views on regulatory options for mandating the guardrails. The
consultation closed on 4 October 2024. The
[consultation page](https://consult.industry.gov.au/ai-mandatory-guardrails)
now states that the Australian Government "will not proceed at this time with
previous proposals to introduce mandatory guardrails for AI development and
deployment", and that feedback on the paper informed the National AI Plan.

The [National AI Plan](/policies/national-ai-plan-2025), published on
2 December 2025, is a strategic plan, not legislation. It builds on existing,
largely technology-neutral laws, leaves regulators responsible for
AI-related harms in their areas, and includes establishing an AI Safety
Institute.

That position covers AI in general. It does not rule out law on particular
uses. The Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet's
[consultation on AI infrastructure](/policies/pmc-ai-infrastructure-consultation-2026),
which closed on 9 October 2026, states that the Government will work with
the states and territories on mandatory standards for data centres and AI
training, and then plans to legislate them.

## Which laws already apply to AI?

The existing laws the National AI Plan builds on apply to AI as they apply to
any other technology. The register tracks the guidance regulators issue on
how they apply to AI.

Privacy law is the clearest example. From 10 December 2026, an entity covered
by the Australian Privacy Principles must describe in its privacy policy the
kinds of personal information and the kinds of decisions involved when it uses
personal information in computer programs to make, or substantially and
directly inform, decisions that could reasonably be expected to significantly
affect an individual's rights or interests. The Office of the
Australian Information Commissioner's
[guidance on APP 1.7–1.9](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)
treats "computer program" broadly, covering rule-based automation as well as
machine learning and generative AI.

## Which laws were written for AI?

A few instruments target AI directly.

- **Online safety.** The eSafety Commissioner registered six
  [industry codes](/policies/esafety-genai-industry-codes) under the Online
  Safety Act on 9 September 2025, and they took effect on 9 March 2026. The
  register records that they include obligations for generative AI services
  and AI companion chatbots.
- **Electoral advertising in South Australia.** Amendments to the Electoral
  Act 1985 that commenced on 10 October 2025
  [prohibit distributing an AI-generated electoral advertisement](/policies/sa-electoral-ai-generated-advertisements)
  that depicts a simulated real person doing something they did not do,
  subject to defences, and require disclosure statements on prescribed
  AI-generated electoral advertisements.
- **Simulated depictions in South Australia.** Amendments to the Summary
  Offences Act 1953 that commenced on 3 November 2025
  [create offences](/policies/sa-summary-offences-ai-generated-depictions)
  for creating or distributing humiliating, degrading or invasive depictions
  of simulated real people generated by AI or digital technology.

## How do governments regulate their own use of AI?

This is the most developed layer. Every Australian government has a policy,
framework or guidance for how its own agencies use AI. These instruments bind
public servants and agencies, not the private sector.

In June 2024, Data and Digital Ministers agreed the
[National framework for the assurance of AI in government](/policies/national-framework-assurance-ai-government).
It is a joint approach by the Australian, state and territory governments,
built on five cornerstones, with practices that apply Australia's AI Ethics
Principles. Each government has its own instrument.

- **Commonwealth.** The
  [Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2),
  version 2.0, took effect on 15 December 2025 for non-corporate Commonwealth
  entities, with specified exceptions. It requires accountable officials,
  transparency statements, AI use-case registers, impact assessments and
  staff training. The Digital Transformation Agency's
  [AI technical standard](/policies/ai-technical-standard) sets out best
  practice for the end-to-end design, development, deployment and use of AI
  systems.
- **New South Wales.** The
  [NSW AI Operational Policy](/policies/nsw-ai-operational-policy) sets
  mandatory requirements for agencies, including registering AI use cases,
  applying the NSW AI Assessment Framework when registration requires it, and
  referring high- and critical-risk uses to the AI Review Committee.
- **Victoria.** An
  [administrative guideline](/policies/victoria-genai-guideline-vps) made in
  November 2024 sets minimum requirements for the safe and responsible use of
  generative AI in the Victorian Public Sector.
- **Queensland.** The mandated
  [AI governance policy](/policies/queensland-ai-governance-policy),
  version 2.0.0, effective from August 2026, requires covered agencies to
  establish AI governance with regard to ISO 38507 and to prepare a
  Foundational AI Risk Assessment (FAIRA) or an agreed equivalent. A shorter
  FAIRA LITE may be used for low-risk uses such as trials.
- **Western Australia.** The
  [WA Government AI Policy and Assurance Framework](/policies/wa-ai-policy-assurance-framework)
  requires each entity to nominate an AI Accountable Officer and to
  self-assess any system or project driven by AI or automated decision making.
  An assessment goes to the WA Government AI Advisory Board when residual
  risk is mid-range or higher, Digital Capability Fund money is involved, or
  total cost exceeds $5 million.
- **South Australia.** The
  [Artificial Intelligence Ethics Policy](/policies/sa-artificial-intelligence-ethics-policy),
  approved on 10 October 2025, is mandatory across the public sector and
  requires agencies to align AI-enabled systems with the AI Ethics Principles
  and the national assurance framework.
- **Tasmania.** The
  [Guidance for the use of AI in Tasmanian Government](/policies/tas-guidance-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-government),
  released in September 2024, gives agencies seven recommendations aligned
  with the national assurance framework.
- **Australian Capital Territory.** The
  [ACT Government AI Policy](/policies/act-ai-policy), version 1.0 of
  May 2025, sets principles, governance roles and lifecycle requirements,
  supported by the ACT AI Assurance Framework.
- **Northern Territory.** The
  [AI Assurance Framework](/policies/nt-artificial-intelligence-assurance-framework),
  published in November 2025, sets ethics principles, a quick risk assessment
  and an assurance process, with stronger controls for medium- and high-risk
  AI.

## What guidance applies to business?

Outside government, most national guidance is voluntary.
[Australia's AI Ethics Principles](/policies/australias-ai-ethics-framework),
first published in November 2019, set out eight voluntary principles. The
National AI Centre's [Guidance for AI Adoption](/policies/guidance-for-ai-adoption),
first published on 21 October 2025, defines six essential practices across
accountability, impacts, risk management, transparency, testing and
monitoring, and human control. It updates and simplifies the
[Voluntary AI Safety Standard](/policies/voluntary-ai-safety-standard) of
September 2024, evolving its ten guardrails into six practices. The register
marks the Standard as superseded.

## What do courts and tribunals require?

Courts and tribunals set rules for anyone who uses generative AI in
proceedings before them, including people without a lawyer.

- The Federal Court's
  [GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai) applies to everyone who appears
  before or files documents with the Court, and states that disclosure of AI
  use may be required.
- The Supreme Court of New South Wales's
  [Practice Note SC Gen 23](/policies/nsw-supreme-court-sc-gen-23),
  which commenced on 3 February 2025, prohibits using generative AI to
  generate the content of affidavits and witness statements, and requires the
  Court's prior leave to use it for expert reports.
- From 20 October 2026, the Fair Work Commission's
  [guidance note](/policies/fwc-genai-guidance-note) requires anyone who uses
  generative AI to prepare a document for a Commission case to disclose it and
  to state that a person has checked the document.

The rules differ between courts. Policai tracks them on the
[court guidance page](/courts).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the practical question for most organisations is not
whether an AI Act applies, but which existing duties their AI use triggers.
The privacy transparency duty that starts on 10 December 2026 is the
broadest new one, because it turns on the use of computer programs in
significant decisions rather than on the label "AI".

For public bodies the picture is the reverse. Each government has its own
policy, and the details differ: who is accountable, which risk assessment
applies and when a central body reviews a use case. A supplier working with
several governments meets a different process in each, even though all of
them refer back to the same national assurance framework.
