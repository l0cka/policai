The [Criminal Code Amendment (Deepfake Sexual Material) Act 2024](/policies/federal-criminal-code-amendment-deepfake-sexual-material-act-2024) received Royal Assent on 2 September 2024 and commenced the next day, 3 September 2024 ([Act No. 78, 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). It applies to material transmitted after commencement, whether the material was created or altered before or after that date ([Act, Schedule 1 item 7](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)).

## What it says

The Act repeals section 474.17A of the Criminal Code and the definition of "private sexual material", and substitutes a new section 474.17A ([Act, Schedule 1 items 1 and 5](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). Under it, a person commits an offence if they use a carriage service to transmit material of another person who is, or appears to be, 18 or older, the material depicts or appears to depict that person in a sexual pose or sexual activity or shows specified intimate body parts, and the person knows the other person does not consent to the transmission or is reckless as to whether they consent ([section 474.17A(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). The penalty is imprisonment for 6 years ([section 474.17A(1)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)).

The provision that makes it a deepfake law is subsection (2): it is irrelevant whether the material is unaltered or "has been created, or altered in any way, using technology" ([section 474.17A(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). A note says this includes images, videos or audio "edited or entirely created using digital technology (including artificial intelligence), generating a realistic but false depiction of the person", and gives "deepfakes" as an example ([section 474.17A(2), note](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). "Transmit" includes making available, publishing, distributing, advertising and promoting, and being reckless as to consent includes not giving any thought to whether the person is consenting ([section 474.17A(4) and (5)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)).

The offence does not apply where transmission is necessary for, or assists, law enforcement or compliance monitoring, is for court or tribunal proceedings, or is for a genuine medical or scientific purpose ([section 474.17A(3)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). Nor does it apply where a reasonable person would consider the transmission acceptable, having regard to listed factors including the nature of the material, the circumstances, the vulnerability of the person depicted, the effect on their privacy and the relationship between the two people ([section 474.17A(3)(d)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). The defendant bears an evidential burden for these exceptions ([section 474.17A(3), note](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). Material depicting a person who is, or appears to be, under 18 is dealt with by the separate child abuse material offences ([section 474.17A(1), note](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)).

New section 474.17AA creates two aggravated offences, each carrying 7 years' imprisonment ([section 474.17AA](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)):

- committing the base offence after 3 or more civil penalty orders were made against the person for contraventions of section 75(1) of the Online Safety Act 2021, or of section 91 of that Act relating to removal notices given under section 89; and
- committing the base offence where the person was responsible for creating or altering the material.

Sections 474.17AB and 474.17B deal with double jeopardy, setting aside a conviction if the underlying civil penalty orders are overturned, and alternative verdicts where an aggravated offence is not proven ([Act, Schedule 1 items 5 and 6](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)).

## Who it affects

The offence applies to any person who uses a carriage service to transmit the material, which on the Act's definition of "transmit" includes making it available or publishing it ([section 474.17A(1) and (4)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)). The higher penalty for creating or altering the material reaches people who generate deepfakes, but only where they also commit the transmission offence ([section 474.17AA(5)](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00078/asmade/text)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the Act is technology neutral by design: it does not define a deepfake or regulate the tools that make one, but removes any argument that synthetic material falls outside the offence. Its reach stops at transmission over a carriage service. Creating a sexual deepfake that is never shared is not caught by this offence, and creation matters only as an aggravating factor. The link to the Online Safety Act civil penalty scheme means a pattern of civil contraventions can lift a later criminal penalty. The Act places no duties on AI developers or platforms as such.

The Act is a criminal law response that does not give the person depicted a removal pathway or a right to sue. The [My Face, My Rights Bill](/policies/federal-online-safety-other-legislation-amendment-my-face-my-rights-bill-2025), a private senator's bill that is not law, proposes both for deepfakes more broadly, so the two instruments address different parts of the same harm.
