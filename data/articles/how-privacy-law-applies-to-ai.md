Does Australian privacy law apply to artificial intelligence (AI)? Yes. There is no separate AI privacy law, but the Privacy Act 1988 applies whenever an AI system collects, uses or discloses personal information. The privacy regulator has explained how, and a new duty to explain decisions made or informed by computer programs applies from 10 December 2026. This guide answers the common questions and links to the register records behind each answer.

## Does the Privacy Act cover AI?

Yes. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says the Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) "apply to all uses of AI involving personal information, including where information is used to train, test or use an AI system" ([OAIC deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)).

The Act applies to Australian Government agencies, to organisations with annual turnover above $3 million, and to some others ([OAIC developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

The OAIC set out its view in two guidance documents published on 21 October 2024. One is for organisations that [use commercial AI products](/policies/oaic-guidance-commercial-ai-products). The other is for developers that [build or train generative AI models](/policies/oaic-guidance-developing-training-genai). The guidance explains how existing law applies. It does not create new law.

## Is what an AI system says about me personal information?

It can be. Both OAIC documents say personal information includes "inferred, incorrect or artificially generated information produced by AI models (such as hallucinations and deepfakes)" about a person who is identified or reasonably identifiable ([OAIC deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products); [OAIC developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

If an organisation uses AI to generate or infer personal information, "this is a collection of personal information and must comply with APP 3" ([OAIC deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)). APP 3 sets the rules on when an organisation may collect personal information.

## Can a business put customer data into an AI tool?

Only within the usual rules. The OAIC says entering personal information into an AI system is a use or disclosure under APP 6. That needs the information's original purpose, consent, or a secondary use the person would reasonably expect ([OAIC deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)).

The same guidance says:

- checking an AI product before use "should not amount to a 'set and forget' approach";
- privacy policies should explain AI use, and public chatbots should be identified as AI; and
- as best practice, organisations should not enter personal information, "and particularly sensitive information", into publicly available generative AI tools.

## Can public data be used to train AI?

Not automatically. The OAIC says "Just because data is publicly available or otherwise accessible does not mean it can legally be used to train or fine-tune generative AI models or systems" ([OAIC developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)). Web scraping "may constitute a covert and therefore unfair means of collection", depending on the circumstances.

Sensitive information, including many photographs and recordings of people, generally needs consent. Where training on data already held was not a purpose of collecting it, the OAIC says a reasonably expected secondary use "will be difficult to establish" in many cases ([OAIC developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

## What changes on 10 December 2026?

The [Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](/policies/federal-privacy-and-other-legislation-amendment-act-2024) added APP 1.7 to 1.9. From 10 December 2026, an organisation or agency "must include information in their privacy policy where they have arranged for a computer program to make or inform decisions that significantly affect individuals' rights or interests" ([OAIC announcement](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)).

The policy must state the kinds of personal information the programs use and the kinds of decisions involved ([record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)). The OAIC's [fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF) reads "computer program" broadly. It covers rule-based processes, machine learning, generative AI, and even "software, apps or word-processing tools". A decision may be in scope "even where a computer program output does not replace the entire decision-making process or is subject to human review".

The fact sheet lists decisions generally in scope, including recruitment screening, loan and credit approvals, insurance eligibility, eligibility for government benefits or housing assistance, and facial recognition watchlist matching in stores or stadiums.

## What else did the 2024 changes add?

The same Act created a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, which commenced on 10 June 2025. It also provided for a Children's Online Privacy Code and gave the Information Commissioner new enforcement powers ([record](/policies/federal-privacy-and-other-legislation-amendment-act-2024)). None of these is limited to AI, but each can apply to AI systems that handle personal information.

## Are there proposals on deepfakes?

A private senator's bill, the [My Face, My Rights Bill 2025](/policies/federal-online-safety-other-legislation-amendment-my-face-my-rights-bill-2025), would add a right to sue under the Privacy Act for the wrongful use or disclosure of deepfake material of a person's face or voice. It was introduced in the Senate on 24 November 2025 and is not law ([record](/policies/federal-online-safety-other-legislation-amendment-my-face-my-rights-bill-2025)).

## What about state governments?

Victoria's public sector has its own privacy law, the Privacy and Data Protection Act 2014, and its own regulator ([record](/policies/vic-ovic-public-generative-ai-guidance)). The Office of the Victorian Information Commissioner (OVIC) says public sector organisations "should ensure their staff, contracted service providers and other personnel do not enter personal information into publicly available Generative AI tools" ([OVIC guidance](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/privacy/resources-for-organisations/use-of-personal-information-with-publicly-available-generative-ai-tools-in-the-victorian-public-sector)). It says doing so "will likely be a contravention of the Information Privacy Principles" in that Act ([OVIC guidance](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/privacy/resources-for-organisations/use-of-personal-information-with-publicly-available-generative-ai-tools-in-the-victorian-public-sector)).

For AI tools bought at an enterprise level, OVIC expects organisations to assess their information security and carry out privacy impact assessments before they adopt the tool ([OVIC enterprise guidance](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/privacy/resources-for-organisations/use-of-enterprise-generative-ai-tools-in-the-victorian-public-sector); [record](/policies/vic-ovic-enterprise-generative-ai-guidance)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the central point is that privacy law follows the information, not the technology. An AI system's inputs, its training data and what it says about a person can all be personal information, so the same APPs apply at each step.

The 10 December 2026 duty is the broadest new obligation, because it turns on whether a decision significantly affects a person, not on whether the program is labelled AI. On the OAIC's reading, a person approving the result does not by itself take the decision out of scope. An organisation that cannot yet list where computer programs feed significant decisions has the most work to do before that date.
