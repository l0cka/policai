What rules apply when a bank, insurer, superannuation trustee or market participant uses artificial intelligence (AI)? Australia has no AI law written for financial services. The two financial regulators apply the powers they already have instead. The Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) has said what it expects of the entities it supervises, and the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC) has amended its binding rules for trading algorithms. General laws, such as the Privacy Act 1988, apply as well. This guide explains each and links to the register records behind it.

## Is there an AI law for financial services?

No. The Australian Government "will not proceed at this time with previous proposals to introduce mandatory guardrails for AI development and deployment" ([consultation page](https://consult.industry.gov.au/ai-mandatory-guardrails)). Its [National AI Plan](/policies/national-ai-plan-2025) relies on existing technology-neutral laws and sector regulators.

For financial firms, that means the regulators' existing frameworks. APRA describes its prudential framework as "technology and vendor agnostic" ([APRA letter](https://www.apra.gov.au/apra-letter-to-industry-on-artificial-intelligence-ai)).

## What does APRA expect?

On 30 April 2026 APRA wrote to all the entities it regulates, calling for "a step-change in how banks, insurers and superannuation trustees manage AI-related risks" ([APRA letter](https://www.apra.gov.au/apra-letter-to-industry-on-artificial-intelligence-ai)). The letter followed a review of selected large banks, insurers and superannuation trustees in late 2025 ([record](/policies/apra-ai-industry-letter-2026)).

APRA found that "assurance practices are not keeping pace with the scale, speed and complexity of AI". It expects boards to understand AI well enough to set direction and to provide "effective challenge and oversight" ([APRA letter](https://www.apra.gov.au/apra-letter-to-industry-on-artificial-intelligence-ai)).

The letter sets minimum expectations in four areas ([APRA letter](https://www.apra.gov.au/apra-letter-to-industry-on-artificial-intelligence-ai)):

- **Security.** Controls over agentic and autonomous workflows, timely patching, security testing of AI-generated code, and a fallback process where AI supports critical operations.
- **Governance.** A list of the AI tools and uses in the business, ownership and accountability across the AI lifecycle, and "human involvement for high-risk decisions and accountability".
- **Suppliers.** A map of the AI supply chain, including the supplier's own suppliers, and credible substitution, portability or exit arrangements for critical AI providers.
- **Assurance.** Ongoing monitoring that fits the use, and risk and audit teams able to check AI systems independently.

APRA says that where entities fail to manage AI risks adequately, it "will take stronger supervisory action and, where appropriate, pursue enforcement" ([APRA letter](https://www.apra.gov.au/apra-letter-to-industry-on-artificial-intelligence-ai)).

## What do the regulators say about frontier AI?

In August 2026 APRA and ASIC published a joint paper on nine industry roundtables attended by more than 380 entities ([record](/policies/asic-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-2026); [p 1](https://download.asic.gov.au/media/imunrchk/asic-and-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-roundatable.pdf)). The regulators expect entities "to take action now" ([p 3](https://download.asic.gov.au/media/imunrchk/asic-and-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-roundatable.pdf)). The register summarises the steps the paper names: strengthen cyber basics, make escalation decisions before a crisis, test response and recovery, map critical suppliers and fallback options, keep human oversight of AI used for defence, and work with the rest of the sector ([record](/policies/asic-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-2026)). The paper points to two existing APRA standards, CPS 230 Operational Risk Management and CPS 234 Information Security ([p 4](https://download.asic.gov.au/media/imunrchk/asic-and-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-roundatable.pdf)).

APRA's letter also directs regulated entities to the Australian Signals Directorate's (ASD) [guidance on frontier AI models](/policies/asd-frontier-ai-models-cyber-security-2026). ASD says these models make software flaws cheaper and easier to find and exploit. It advises organisations to keep strong cyber security baselines, reduce exposed attack paths, patch more often and use AI for defence where appropriate ([record](/policies/asd-frontier-ai-models-cyber-security-2026)).

## What rules apply to AI trading?

ASIC has amended its market integrity rules for securities and futures markets to set specific duties for trading algorithms, including those that use AI and machine learning ([ASIC media release](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-226mr-asic-strengthens-ai-trading-safeguards-and-streamlines-market-integrity-rules)). They are rules made under the Corporations Act 2001, not guidance ([record](/policies/asic-market-integrity-rules-ai-trading-2026)).

A trading algorithm is one that automatically sets one or more details of an order "with limited or no human intervention" ([securities instrument, item 33](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L01223/asmade/2026-09-17/text/original/pdf)). Under the amended rules, a trading participant must govern and test each algorithm before use, be able to stop one immediately, monitor its trading and keep records for seven years ([securities instrument, rr 5.6.3 to 5.6.3B](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L01223/asmade/2026-09-17/text/original/pdf)). ASIC's explanatory statement calls the stop control the "kill switch" ([ES para 52](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L01223/asmade/2026-09-17/es/original/pdf)).

The rule against false or misleading trading now also reaches orders that have that effect, not only orders meant to. ASIC says this covers orders from agentic AI systems "whether or not a person intended that effect" ([ES para 29](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2026L01223/asmade/2026-09-17/es/original/pdf)).

The rules commence 18 months after registration. The register records a Federal Register start date of 17 March 2028 for the securities rules, while ASIC gives 18 March 2028 ([record](/policies/asic-market-integrity-rules-ai-trading-2026); [CS 63](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/consultations/cs-63-proposed-updates-to-rg-265-and-rg-266-and-withdrawal-of-rg-241)). ASIC consulted on updated guidance, with comments due by 5 November 2026 ([CS 63](https://www.asic.gov.au/regulatory-resources/find-a-document/consultations/cs-63-proposed-updates-to-rg-265-and-rg-266-and-withdrawal-of-rg-241)).

## What does privacy law add?

From 10 December 2026, an entity covered by the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) must say in its privacy policy when it uses computer programs to make, or help make, decisions that significantly affect people ([record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)). The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner's (OAIC) fact sheet lists loan and credit approvals and insurance eligibility among the decisions generally in scope ([fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF)). In the OAIC's reading, human review of an output does not by itself take a decision out of scope ([record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)).

The OAIC's [guidance on commercial AI products](/policies/oaic-guidance-commercial-ai-products) also applies. It says entering personal information into an AI system is a use or disclosure under APP 6, and that AI-generated or inferred personal information "is a collection of personal information and must comply with APP 3" ([OAIC guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, a financial firm faces three kinds of AI rule at once. APRA's letter creates no new prudential standard, but APRA will supervise against its reading of the existing ones. ASIC's trading rules and the privacy duty are law, with fixed start dates.

For most firms the first practical step is the same under all three: know where AI is used. APRA asks for an inventory of AI tools and uses, ASIC's rules need every trading algorithm governed and tested, and the privacy duty needs a firm to know which significant decisions involve a computer program. The expectations APRA reports few large entities met, such as tested exit plans for critical AI providers and ongoing model monitoring, are likely to take longest.
