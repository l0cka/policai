On 30 September 2026 the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) published [guidance](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance) on a new transparency obligation in Australian Privacy Principle (APP) 1. From 10 December 2026, APP entities "must include information in their privacy policy where they have arranged for a computer program to make or inform decisions that significantly affect individuals' rights or interests" ([OAIC announcement](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)).

## What it says

The obligation was introduced by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 ([OAIC announcement](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)). Under APP 1.7, an entity is in scope when three conditions are met ([fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF)):

- it has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing substantially and directly related to making one;
- the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual's rights or interests; and
- personal information about the individual is used in the program's operation.

APP 1.8 then requires the privacy policy to state the kinds of personal information used in those programs, the kinds of decisions made solely by them, and the kinds of decisions for which they do a thing substantially and directly related to the decision ([OAIC announcement](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)).

The [fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF) defines "computer program" to include pre-programmed rule-based processes, AI and machine learning, "software, apps or word-processing tools" and generative AI, including chatbots. A decision may be in scope "even where a computer program output does not replace the entire decision-making process or is subject to human review". The OAIC considers that machine learning or generative AI outputs used in significant decisions "would generally fall within scope of the transparency obligation unless subject to extensive human oversight and control".

The OAIC says commercially sensitive information and trade secrets are excluded, relying on the explanatory memorandum addendum rather than the APP text. Information that would expose an entity "to ridicule, embarrassment or public criticism" is not ([fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF)).

The OAIC also published a supplementary fact sheet for government agencies and a flowchart, and updated its APP 1 Guidelines ([OAIC announcement](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/new-resources-on-transparency-for-use-of-ai-and-automated-decision-making)).

## Who it affects

The [fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF) lists decisions generally in scope, including recruitment screening, loan and credit approvals, insurance eligibility, eligibility for government benefits or housing assistance, AI reports used to rank staff or set pay, and facial recognition watchlist matching in stores or stadiums.

The OAIC "generally expects" the obligation to stay with the entity that uses personal information to make the decision. Vendors of third-party software "should provide clear, high-level information" so their customers can write their policies. Entities unsure whether they are in scope "should take a cautious approach" and include the information ([fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the obligation turns on the decision's effect on a person, not on the technology. Example 1 in the [fact sheet](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF) puts a spreadsheet formula that ranks clients for health and aged care in scope.

On the OAIC's reading, human sign-off alone does not take a decision out of scope. In its [Example 3, on remuneration](https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0021/269013/APP-1.7-1.9-Transparency-Obligation-Fact-Sheet.PDF), a director approves every outcome and the AI recommendation is still in scope. Only further steps, such as interrogating outputs, using additional evidence and documenting why the decision departed from the recommendation, "could indicate" otherwise.

Entities using vendor tools depend on vendor information to describe them. The fact sheet requires grouped descriptions to be "meaningful to a reasonable person" but, beyond general guidance and its examples, sets no fixed level of detail.
