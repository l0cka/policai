Public Record Office Victoria (PROV) publishes [guidance on artificial intelligence](/policies/victoria-ai-recordkeeping-guidance) for "capturing and managing records generated by or using AI technologies", in a page dated 4 June 2026 ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)). The register holds it as active Victorian guidance, dated to that update ([record](/policies/victoria-ai-recordkeeping-guidance)).

## What it says

The guidance starts from the nature of the systems. It describes AI systems as "dynamic, probabilistic, and constantly evolving", and recommends that, to maintain a "full and accurate record", public offices document "more than just the final decision or action": the code, the environment, the intermediate logic and the processes that produced it ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)). It says records of the technologies and processes matter because they affect access to the record, the context in which it is understood and its integrity as evidence ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)).

It then sets out what records need to show for different kinds of AI ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)):

- **Agentic AI, automated decision-making systems, expert systems and robotic process automation:** that the decision or action was lawful, followed appropriate procedures and lines of authorisation, involved no bias or addressed it appropriately, and was based on accurate and current information;
- **Generative AI, large language models and multimodal foundation models:** that the content was fact checked and is accurate, current and appropriate, and is in line with relevant legislation and regulation; and
- **Virtual agents, chatbots and natural language systems:** the same checks, plus that the content produced is authorised and valid.

A seven-point starting list for records management programs follows ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)). It asks public offices to identify and document each AI system they use, including its purpose, type, data sources, identified harms and risks, controls, and what cannot be documented, such as "black box technologies". It asks them to record how each system has worked over time, for example through automated logging; to decide and document which content and actions AI may produce or take and where a human must be included; to attribute generative AI output and set lines of delegation and accountability for each system; to run an ongoing assessment and monitoring program; and to retain AI-generated records for the minimum periods in the relevant retention and disposal authority ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)).

The guidance also pairs each of Australia's AI Ethics Principles with documentation questions, maps recordkeeping onto the National AI Centre's six practices, including keeping an AI register, and lists external documentation tools, among them the [NSW AI Assessment Framework](/policies/nsw-artificial-intelligence-assessment-framework) and the Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)). On communication with the public, it suggests acknowledging when AI is used and notifying people who interact with AI, with information on opting out and requesting review ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)).

## Who it affects

The guidance is addressed to Victorian public offices. It says that under the Public Records Act 1973 "the head of the public office is ultimately accountable" for full and accurate records, including records of AI technologies, their use and impact, and that those records must meet PROV's recordkeeping standards and its AI Technologies and Recordkeeping Policy ([PROV](https://prov.vic.gov.au/recordkeeping-government/a-z-topics/AI)).

It connects to the Victorian public sector's [generative AI guideline](/policies/victoria-genai-guideline-vps), which sets minimum requirements for generative AI use, lists the Public Records Act 1973 among the obligations that apply, and points staff to PROV for advice on AI and recordkeeping, including its recordkeeping policy ([Victorian Government](https://www.vic.gov.au/administrative-guideline-safe-responsible-use-gen-ai-vps)). A public service body or public entity that operates inconsistently with that guideline must give written reasons to the Secretary of the Department of Premier and Cabinet ([Victorian Government](https://www.vic.gov.au/administrative-guideline-safe-responsible-use-gen-ai-vps)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, most government AI guidance on the register asks whether a system should be used and how it should be assured. PROV's guidance asks a different question: what evidence must exist afterwards. That turns principles such as transparency and accountability into something an auditor, a court or a freedom of information applicant could later test against the file.

In Policai's analysis, the most demanding line is the recommendation to keep the code, environment and intermediate logic, not just the outcome. For systems bought as services, agencies may not hold that material, and the guidance's own instruction to document what "cannot be documented" acknowledges the gap rather than closing it. That makes contract terms on access to logs and documentation as much a recordkeeping question as a procurement one.

In Policai's analysis, the guidance is the records counterpart to the [generative AI guideline](/policies/victoria-genai-guideline-vps): the guideline sets the rules for using the tools, and PROV sets out what must be kept to show those rules were followed. Its listing of the [NSW AI Assessment Framework](/policies/nsw-artificial-intelligence-assessment-framework) as a documentation tool, with a note that such tools may need tailoring for Victoria, also shows one state's assurance instrument being offered for use in another.
