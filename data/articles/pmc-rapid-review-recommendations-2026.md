On 10 November 2026 the Prime Minister released the [18 recommendations](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident-recommendations) of the Rapid Review into Australian Government arrangements for an AI-driven cyber incident. The government said it accepts them and has commenced work to implement them ([PM release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/strengthening-australia-against-ai-driven-cyber-incidents)). A separate [release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/new-australian-ai-safety-and-security-protections) that day said it will legislate mandatory safety and security protections for frontier AI companies through Australian AI Standards legislation, enforced by a proposed new regulator for AI safety. Policai's [earlier note](/analysis/pmc-rapid-review-ai-driven-cyber-incident-2026) covered the terms of reference after the [24 September 2026 announcement](https://www.pmc.gov.au/domestic-policy/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident).

## What it says

The recommendations release says the review was commissioned in September after OpenAI informed the government that a "rogue AI agent had infiltrated government websites, including the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service Portal". It describes the incident as unacceptable and the disclosure as inadequate ([PM release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/strengthening-australia-against-ai-driven-cyber-incidents)). The review was undertaken by the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet with the National Cyber Security Coordinator, the Office of AI, the Australian Signals Directorate (ASD), the Australian AI Safety Institute and Services Australia ([PM release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/strengthening-australia-against-ai-driven-cyber-incidents)).

PM&C's [recommendations](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident-recommendations) say the review examined the OpenAI incident affecting Services Australia and made 18 recommendations "to ensure Australia is better prepared for AI incidents of greater scale, complexity and consequence". They include strengthening engagement with frontier AI developers (recommendations 1 and 2); considering "mandatory, time-bound AI safety reporting, including but not limited to misaligned behaviour or agentic cyber incidents", and exploring protections for timely reporting analogous to existing Limited Use protections (recommendations 3 and 4); international standards work and the AI Safety Institute's future role (recommendations 5 to 7); and stronger government incident management and cyber practice (recommendations 9 to 18), including asking agencies responsible for national crisis plans to consider AI's impact and report to the Crisis Arrangements Committee Chairs by the end of 2026 (recommendation 13).

Recommendation 8 asks the Attorney-General's Department, consulting the Australian Federal Police, the Department of Home Affairs and other agencies, to consider criminal-law reform, including "the application of criminal penalties for egregious regulatory non-compliance; and/or a standalone 'failure to prevent' offence which would criminalise instances where the company is responsible for developing an AI model that engages in harmful conduct, and the company fails to take reasonable steps to prevent that harm" ([recommendations](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident-recommendations)).

Separately, the government said mandatory protections will be legislated through the Australian AI Standards and "apply to all frontier AI companies that wish to operate in Australia" ([joint release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/new-australian-ai-safety-and-security-protections)). It describes frontier AI as "the most sophisticated models at the cutting edge of current technological capability". Companies would have to maintain security controls across development and deployment; identify and manage serious risks; allow independent evaluation of their models; notify the government of incidents rapidly; maintain and publish safety and security frameworks; and respond to updated standards. A "new targeted regulator for AI safety" would enforce these obligations, working with the Australian AI Safety Institute, and "would be able to enforce significant penalties" for non-compliance. The government said the protections are aligned with interim report recommendations from the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, and that further legislative details will become available "in due course" ([joint release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/new-australian-ai-safety-and-security-protections)).

## Who it affects

The recommendations are not legislation. The government has accepted them and said implementation has begun ([PM release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/strengthening-australia-against-ai-driven-cyber-incidents)). Recommendations 3 and 8 ask it only to consider mandatory reporting and criminal-law reform ([recommendations](https://www.pmc.gov.au/resources/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident/rapid-review-australian-government-arrangements-ai-driven-cyber-incident-recommendations)). All 18 recommendations are addressed to government. Recommendations 1 to 4 and 8 concern frontier AI developers, and recommendations 9 to 18 concern the Commonwealth's own incident management and cyber practice. Only the separate standards announcement proposes obligations on companies. The proposed regulator and the six obligations are not yet law: the joint release names no penalty amounts, commencement date or bill text ([joint release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/new-australian-ai-safety-and-security-protections)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the 10 November announcements end the review's information-gathering stage without creating reporting duties, criminal offences or a regulator. Accepting a recommendation to consider mandatory reporting, or a "failure to prevent" offence, is not the same as enacting either. The six frontier obligations and the proposed regulator sit beside the Rapid Review, not inside its numbered list, and the joint release leaves open how that regulator would relate to the Office of AI, ASD and the AI Safety Institute.

In Policai's analysis, recommendations 3 and 4 address the delayed-notification problem identified in Policai's [earlier note](/analysis/pmc-rapid-review-ai-driven-cyber-incident-2026). They sit alongside two other proposals that are also not law: Andrew Gee's [AI Kill Switch and Data Centre Control Bill 2026](/policies/federal-ai-kill-switch-and-data-centre-control-bill-2026), which would impose critical incident reporting obligations on advanced AI systems, and ASD's [alert on risks of AI misalignment](/policies/asd-risks-ai-misalignment-2026), published on 25 September 2026.
