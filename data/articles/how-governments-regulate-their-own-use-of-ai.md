What rules apply when an Australian government department uses artificial intelligence (AI)? Every Australian government has its own policy, framework or guidance. These instruments govern agencies and public servants, not businesses or the public. This guide explains what they have in common, how they differ, and what they mean for the people who deal with government or sell to it.

## Who do these rules apply to?

Government agencies and their staff. The Commonwealth policy, for example, is mandatory for non-corporate Commonwealth entities and encouraged for corporate Commonwealth entities, with the Defence portfolio and the National Intelligence Community listed as exceptions ([version 2.1 PDF](https://www.digital.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2026-10/Policy%20for%20the%20responsible%20use%20of%20AI%20in%20government%202_1_1.pdf)). In Policai's reading, businesses are not bound directly, but they meet these rules when they sell AI products or services to an agency.

## Is there a national approach?

Partly. In June 2024, Data and Digital Ministers agreed the [National framework for the assurance of AI in government](/policies/national-framework-assurance-ai-government). It is a joint approach of the Australian, state and territory governments, built on five cornerstones, with practices mapped to Australia's eight AI Ethics Principles. Each government then develops its own policy to suit its own laws and operations ([record](/policies/national-framework-assurance-ai-government)).

## What do Commonwealth agencies have to do?

The [Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2) is the main rule. Version 2.0 took effect on 15 December 2025, and the Digital Transformation Agency (DTA) published "minor updates" as version 2.1 on 21 October 2026 ([DTA policy page](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy)). It requires agencies to have accountable officials, publish transparency statements, keep a register of AI use cases, carry out AI use-case impact assessments and train staff ([record](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2)).

Agencies must sort each use case into categories. One is "decision-making and administrative action", which covers AI used "to make, or substantially and directly inform, a decision or administrative action that could affect the rights or interests of an individual or entity". Its examples include "risk scoring, prioritisation recommendations, case triage, benefit assessment support and recommended enforcement actions for human decision makers" ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)).

For existing use cases not yet assessed, agencies "must determine whether they are in scope of this policy and apply all relevant policy actions by 30 April 2027" ([impact assessment page](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/ai-use-case-impact-assessment)). A use case rated high risk is reported to the agency's accountable official and, once the agency decides to deploy it, to the DTA ([impact assessment page](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/ai-use-case-impact-assessment)).

Several other federal instruments sit around the policy:

- The DTA's [AI technical standard](/policies/ai-technical-standard) sets requirements across an AI system's life, from design to retirement.
- The [AI Reference Architecture](/policies/dta-ai-reference-architecture) helps agencies design AI systems. The DTA says "Using the architecture is voluntary" ([media release](https://www.dta.gov.au/media-releases/ai-reference-architecture-new-blueprint-help-government-agencies-design-ai-systems)).
- The [AI Plan for the Australian Public Service](/policies/aps-ai-plan-2025) includes foundational AI training, secure AI tools and Chief AI Officers.
- The Australian Public Service Commission's [principles for AI in recruitment](/policies/ai-in-recruitment) require agencies to preserve merit, fairness, transparency, privacy and human accountability, with implementation expected from 1 June 2026.

Each has its own instrument, and the details differ: what kind of risk assessment applies, and when a use case goes to a central body for review.

A few points stand out in the records:

- **New South Wales.** Agencies must register AI use cases, apply the AI Assessment Framework when registration requires it, train staff and attest to compliance every year ([record](/policies/nsw-ai-operational-policy)). "From 30 September 2026, the AIAF Platform is the authoritative channel for AI use case registration and assessment across NSW Government" ([Identifying AI](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/policy/artificial-intelligence/nsw-artificial-intelligence-assessment-framework/identifying-ai); [record](/policies/nsw-identifying-ai-guidance)).
- **Queensland.** When evaluating AI, agencies "must prepare a Foundational AI risk assessment (FAIRA), or an agreed equivalent" ([policy page](https://www.forgov.qld.gov.au/information-technology/queensland-government-enterprise-architecture-qgea/qgea-directions-and-guidance/qgea-policies-standards-and-guidelines/artificial-intelligence-governance-policy); [record](/policies/queensland-ai-governance-policy)).
- **Western Australia.** Each entity must nominate an AI Accountable Officer, who approves its self-assessments. The AI Advisory Board's recommendations are non-binding ([record](/policies/wa-ai-policy-assurance-framework)).
- **South Australia.** The [AI Ethics Policy](/policies/sa-artificial-intelligence-ethics-policy), approved on 10 October 2025, applies to all public sector agencies.
- **Tasmania.** Tasmania's [instrument](/policies/tas-guidance-for-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-government) is guidance, with seven recommendations for agencies.

## What rules apply to staff using generative AI?

Several governments have separate guidance for tools like chatbots. Victoria's privacy regulator says staff and contractors should not enter personal information into publicly available generative AI tools ([OVIC guidance](https://ovic.vic.gov.au/privacy/resources-for-organisations/use-of-personal-information-with-publicly-available-generative-ai-tools-in-the-victorian-public-sector); [record](/policies/vic-ovic-public-generative-ai-guidance)). Queensland's [fact sheet](/policies/queensland-use-of-generative-ai-in-government) covers privacy and security, accuracy, recordkeeping and human review of outputs. South Australia's [guideline](/policies/sa-guideline-use-of-generative-ai-and-llm-tools) sets expectations on privacy, confidentiality, security and checking outputs.

## What does this mean for suppliers?

Suppliers meet these rules through procurement. NSW publishes [AI procurement essentials](/policies/nsw-ai-procurement-essentials) on assessing AI products, suppliers, risks and contract terms ([record](/policies/nsw-ai-procurement-essentials)). The DTA says agencies can use its reference architecture to compare vendor offerings ([record](/policies/dta-ai-reference-architecture)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, government use is the most developed layer of AI governance in Australia. Each government has its own instrument, and most rest on the same national framework.

The differences matter to anyone working across borders. A supplier selling to several governments meets a different process in each: a FAIRA in Queensland, platform registration in New South Wales, a self-assessment in Western Australia. The Commonwealth's decision-making category means a triage or scoring tool counts as decision-making even when a person signs off the final decision. For the public, the visible parts are the transparency statements and use-case registers that some governments require.
