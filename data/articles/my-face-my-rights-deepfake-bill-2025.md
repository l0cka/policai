The Online Safety and Other Legislation Amendment (My Face, My Rights) Bill 2025 is a private senator's bill sponsored by Senator David Pocock ([Parliament of Australia](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Bills_Legislation/Bills_Search_Results/Result?bId=s1471)). It was introduced in the Senate on 24 November 2025, a second reading debate took place on 25 June 2026, and its status is "Before Senate" ([record](/policies/federal-online-safety-other-legislation-amendment-my-face-my-rights-bill-2025)). It is a proposed law, not an Act.

## What it says

The bill has two parts. Schedule 1 would add a complaints and enforcement scheme for deepfake material to the Online Safety Act 2021, and Schedule 2 would add a new Schedule 3 to the Privacy Act 1988 creating a cause of action for the wrongful use or disclosure of deepfake material ([explanatory memorandum](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/ems/s1471_ems_6b78644a-3a08-4850-911f-b9f9d6ade06e/upload_pdf/EM_25S29.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)). Both schedules would commence the day after Royal Assent ([bill as introduced, s 2](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)).

Material would be deepfake material if it is an image, video or audio that depicts "in a realistic way, an individual's face or voice", and the depiction "is false on account of the material being created or altered entirely, substantially or in a significant respect using technology" ([bill as introduced, s 21A(1)](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)). A note says this covers material created with digital technology, "including artificial intelligence". For the Online Safety Act scheme, the definition excludes cyber-bullying material, cyber-abuse material, intimate images and abhorrent violent material, which stay with their existing parts of the Act ([s 21A(2)](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)). Consent to posting must be "express, voluntary and informed", and for a subject under 16 it comes from a parent or guardian ([s 21A(4)–(5)](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)).

Under the Online Safety Act changes ([bill as introduced, Sch 1](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)):

- **Posting:** an end-user ordinarily resident in Australia who posts deepfake material, aware that it is non-consensual for a subject, would face a civil penalty of 500 penalty units (s 93B).
- **Complaints:** a subject aged 16 or over, or a parent, guardian or authorised person, could complain to the eSafety Commissioner, who may investigate (ss 37A, 37B).
- **Removal notices:** the Commissioner could require a service provider, end-user or hosting provider to remove the material or stop hosting it within 24 hours (ss 93D–93F). Notices to services and hosts would need a prior complaint to the service that was not acted on within 48 hours. Failing to comply would carry a civil penalty of 500 penalty units (s 93G).
- **Other powers:** formal warnings, remedial directions, and published statements about services where non-consensual deepfakes in breach of their terms of use appeared on 2 or more occasions in 12 months (ss 93C, 93H, 93J–93L).

The new Privacy Act Schedule 3 would give an individual a cause of action in tort if another person uses or discloses deepfake material of them, knowing it was made or altered using technology or reckless as to that, without consent, and the individual suffers detriment or the other person makes a profit ([bill as introduced, Sch 2, cl 7](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)). It would be "actionable without proof of damage". Remedies would include damages, an account of profits, injunctions, apologies and correction orders, but not aggravated damages (cll 10, 12, 13). Proceedings would have to start within 1 year of the plaintiff becoming aware, or 3 years of the use or disclosure, whichever is earlier, with a court able to extend to 6 years (cl 15).

## Who it affects

The Online Safety Act scheme would apply to providers of social media services, relevant electronic services and designated internet services, hosting service providers, and end-users who post material ([bill as introduced, s 93A](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)). The cause of action could be brought by individuals ordinarily resident in Australia against any person who uses or discloses deepfake material of them, and it binds the Crown ([Sch 2, cll 3, 7](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)).

Journalists working under professional standards, government agencies acting in good faith, law enforcement and intelligence agencies, and people under 18 would be exempt from the cause of action ([Sch 2, cll 16–21](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/bills/s1471_first-senate/toc_pdf/25S2920.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)). The explanatory memorandum says the eSafety Commissioner and the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner would implement the bill within existing resources ([explanatory memorandum](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/download/legislation/ems/s1471_ems_6b78644a-3a08-4850-911f-b9f9d6ade06e/upload_pdf/EM_25S29.pdf;fileType=application%2Fpdf)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the bill takes two routes to the same harm. The eSafety route is fast and administrative, and turns on whether material was posted without consent. The court route turns on use or disclosure, which reaches beyond posting online, and on detriment or profit. The profit limb means a commercial use of a person's synthetic face or voice, such as a cloned voice in an advertisement, could ground a claim without any loss to the person depicted.

The definition is framed by what the material shows, not by the tool used to make it. Any realistic false depiction of a face or voice made "using technology" qualifies, so the bill does not depend on a definition of AI.

The bill is a private senator's bill and remains before the Senate. None of it would apply unless it passes both houses and receives Royal Assent ([Parliament of Australia](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Bills_Legislation/Bills_Search_Results/Result?bId=s1471)).
