The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) set out how the Privacy Act 1988 applies to AI in two guidance documents published on 21 October 2024. [Guidance on privacy and the use of commercially available AI products](/policies/oaic-guidance-commercial-ai-products) is for organisations that deploy AI ([OAIC guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)). [Guidance on privacy and developing and training generative AI models](/policies/oaic-guidance-developing-training-genai) is for developers ([OAIC guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)). The OAIC's pages show the deployer guidance as updated on 17 January 2025 and the developer guidance as updated on 23 October 2024.

## What it says

Both documents start from the same position. The deployer guidance says the Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) "apply to all uses of AI involving personal information, including where information is used to train, test or use an AI system" ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)). Both say personal information includes "inferred, incorrect or artificially generated information produced by AI models (such as hallucinations and deepfakes)" where it is about an identified or reasonably identifiable individual ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products); [developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

For organisations using AI products, the deployer guidance says:

- organisations should do due diligence on whether a product suits its intended uses, including whether it has been tested for them, how human oversight can be built in, its privacy and security risks, and who will have access to personal information entered or generated;
- due diligence "should not amount to a 'set and forget' approach", and products should be reviewed and monitored across their lifecycle;
- if AI is used to generate or infer personal information, "this is a collection of personal information and must comply with APP 3";
- entering personal information into an AI system is a use or disclosure under APP 6, so it must be for the primary purpose of collection unless the individual consented or would reasonably expect the related secondary use;
- privacy policies and notices should explain the organisation's use of AI, and public-facing tools such as chatbots should be clearly identified as AI; and
- as a matter of best practice, organisations should not enter personal information, "and particularly sensitive information", into publicly available generative AI tools.

([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products))

For developers, the developer guidance says:

- "Just because data is publicly available or otherwise accessible does not mean it can legally be used to train or fine-tune generative AI models or systems";
- sensitive information generally requires consent, so many photographs or recordings of people "may not be able to be scraped from the web or collected from a third party dataset without establishing consent", and sensitive information collected without consent will generally need to be deleted;
- creating a dataset by web scraping "may constitute a covert and therefore unfair means of collection", depending on the circumstances;
- using personal information already held to train a model, where training was not a primary purpose of collection, needs consent or a secondary use the individual would reasonably expect, and the OAIC says that in many cases this "will be difficult to establish"; and
- where a developer cannot clearly establish that, it should seek consent and offer "a meaningful and informed ability to opt-out".

([developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models))

Both documents also apply APP 10 on accuracy. Organisations must take reasonable steps to ensure accuracy "commensurate with the likely increased level of risk in an AI context", which may include disclaimers, and for developers also high-quality datasets and testing ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products); [developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

## Who it affects

The developer guidance says the Privacy Act applies to Australian Government agencies, organisations with an annual turnover of more than $3 million, and some other organisations. It also applies to acts outside Australia by organisations with an Australian link, such as carrying on business in Australia ([developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

The deployer guidance is aimed at any organisation that supplies or uses an AI system to provide a product or service, including internally. It names chatbots, content-generation tools and productivity assistants for writing, coding, note-taking and transcription ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)). The developer guidance covers any organisation that designs, builds, trains, adapts or combines AI models and applications, including by fine-tuning a model someone else built ([developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

Both documents say they will also help organisations address the 10 voluntary guardrails in the National AI Centre's Voluntary AI Safety Standard, which the OAIC says "does not seek to create new legal obligations" ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products); [developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the guidance matters because it is the privacy regulator's own statement of how existing privacy law reaches AI. The guidance is not law, but it states the OAIC's view of how it reads the APPs, so the points where it uses "must" track existing obligations, while its "should" points describe the OAIC's expectations and best practice.

In Policai's analysis, two positions carry most weight. The first is that scraping publicly available data is not automatically lawful collection, which goes to how many generative models are built. The second is that AI-generated or inferred information about a person, including a hallucination, is a collection of personal information. That brings outputs, not only inputs, within APP 3 and APP 10.

In Policai's analysis, the deployer guidance's transparency points sit alongside the OAIC's later [guidance on APP 1.7 to 1.9](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance), which deals with privacy policy disclosures about automated decisions under obligations that apply from 10 December 2026 ([record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)).
