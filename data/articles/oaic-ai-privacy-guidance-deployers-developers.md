The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) set out how the Privacy Act 1988 applies to AI in two guidance documents published on 21 October 2024. [Guidance on privacy and the use of commercially available AI products](/policies/oaic-guidance-commercial-ai-products) is for organisations that deploy AI, and [guidance on privacy and developing and training generative AI models](/policies/oaic-guidance-developing-training-genai) is for developers ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products); [developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

## What it says

The Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) "apply to all uses of AI involving personal information, including where information is used to train, test or use an AI system" ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)). Both documents say personal information includes "inferred, incorrect or artificially generated information produced by AI models (such as hallucinations and deepfakes)" about an identified or reasonably identifiable individual ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products); [developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

The deployer guidance says ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)):

- due diligence on a product "should not amount to a 'set and forget' approach";
- if AI is used to generate or infer personal information, "this is a collection of personal information and must comply with APP 3";
- entering personal information into an AI system is a use or disclosure under APP 6, needing the primary purpose of collection, consent or a reasonably expected secondary use;
- privacy policies should explain AI use, and public chatbots should be identified as AI; and
- as best practice, organisations should not enter personal information, "and particularly sensitive information", into publicly available generative AI tools.

The developer guidance says ([developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)):

- "Just because data is publicly available or otherwise accessible does not mean it can legally be used to train or fine-tune generative AI models or systems";
- web scraping "may constitute a covert and therefore unfair means of collection", depending on the circumstances;
- sensitive information, including many photographs and recordings of people, generally requires consent; and
- training on personal information already held, where training was not a primary purpose, needs consent or a reasonably expected secondary use. The OAIC says this "will be difficult to establish" in many cases, and where it cannot be established developers should seek consent and offer "a meaningful and informed ability to opt-out".

## Who it affects

The Privacy Act applies to Australian Government agencies, organisations with annual turnover above $3 million and some others, including acts abroad by organisations with an Australian link ([developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)). The deployer guidance covers any organisation that supplies or uses an AI system to provide a product or service, including internally, such as chatbots and productivity assistants ([deployer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-the-use-of-commercially-available-ai-products)). The developer guidance covers organisations that design, build, train, adapt or combine AI models, including by fine-tuning someone else's model ([developer guidance](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/guidance-on-privacy-and-developing-and-training-generative-ai-models)).

## Analysis

This is the privacy regulator's reading of how existing law reaches AI. It is not law: its "must" points track existing obligations, and its "should" points state expectations.

Two positions carry most weight. Scraping public data is not automatically lawful collection, which goes to how many generative models are built. Treating AI-generated or inferred information, hallucinations included, as collected personal information brings outputs, not only inputs, within APP 3.

The transparency points sit alongside the OAIC's later [guidance on APP 1.7 to 1.9](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance) on disclosing automated decisions, under obligations that apply from 10 December 2026 ([record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)).
