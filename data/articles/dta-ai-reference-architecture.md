The Digital Transformation Agency (DTA) published the AI Reference Architecture (AIRA) and a Companion Guide on 30 October 2026 ([media release](https://www.dta.gov.au/media-releases/ai-reference-architecture-new-blueprint-help-government-agencies-design-ai-systems)). The introduction describes AIRA as an architectural foundation for designing, implementing and governing artificial intelligence (AI) across the Australian Government, and says it supports the [AI Plan for the Australian Public Service 2025](/policies/aps-ai-plan-2025) and the [Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2) ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)). The AIRA page marks both accessible PDFs as last updated on 23 October 2026 ([AIRA page](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture)).

## What it says

The release sets out four constructs (a functional stack, communication, trustworthiness and assurance, and human agency, oversight, and control) and five layers in the stack: experience, application and orchestration, intelligence and analytics, data and metadata, and platform and infrastructure ([media release](https://www.dta.gov.au/media-releases/ai-reference-architecture-new-blueprint-help-government-agencies-design-ai-systems)).

The introduction says agencies can use AIRA, after a problem has been defined and validated, to shape a target architecture, compare vendor offerings, and govern a system, including by informing an [AI use case impact assessment](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/ai-use-case-impact-assessment) and tracing requirements in the [AI technical standard](/policies/ai-technical-standard) ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)). It says AIRA does not replace problem-framing guidance, a business case, procurement documentation, a return-on-investment calculation or detailed implementation guidance ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)).

Lucy Poole, Deputy CEO - Digital Strategy, Planning and Performance, says in the release: "Using the architecture is voluntary. It supports agencies to meet applicable requirements, but does not replace them" ([media release](https://www.dta.gov.au/media-releases/ai-reference-architecture-new-blueprint-help-government-agencies-design-ai-systems)).

The Companion Guide sets out four scenarios: enabling AI within an existing vendor platform, designing a new in-house service, procuring an agentic call centre solution, and integrating AI in case management ([Companion Guide](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/companion-guide)). It says the scenarios do not identify a single correct architecture, and it groups control as "govern", "configure and govern", or "design, configure and govern", with "govern" the baseline in every scenario ([Companion Guide](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/companion-guide)).

## Who it affects

The release is addressed to Australian Government agencies ([media release](https://www.dta.gov.au/media-releases/ai-reference-architecture-new-blueprint-help-government-agencies-design-ai-systems)). The introduction's primary audience is people who design, build, deploy, operate and oversee AI-enabled systems, including architects, engineering and data teams, delivery staff, and governance and assurance practitioners ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)). It says leadership and accountable officials use AIRA for strategy, investment, governance and risk, and that procurement and information management use it for sourcing, recordkeeping and provenance ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)). It says AIRA applies across machine learning, generative AI, agentic AI and emerging frontier AI, from vendor-managed services to internally developed models ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, AIRA does not create a duty of its own: Poole says use is voluntary and that the architecture does not replace applicable requirements ([media release](https://www.dta.gov.au/media-releases/ai-reference-architecture-new-blueprint-help-government-agencies-design-ai-systems)). The introduction treats the impact assessment and the [AI technical standard](/policies/ai-technical-standard) as instruments AIRA can inform, and it does not say that using AIRA meets the [Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2) ([introduction](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/introduction)).

In Policai's analysis, the control categories are the part to watch: "govern", the baseline in every scenario, covers evidence, acceptable use, disclosures, obligations, deployment conditions, operating processes and residual risk, and "configure" and "design" add levers ([Companion Guide](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/companion-guide)). The guide expects an agency to build on a scenario from its own objectives, constraints, operating model and risk profile, and the scenarios page does not say that fewer design choices change what the policy or the technical standard requires ([Companion Guide](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai-reference-architecture/companion-guide)).
