The NSW Education Standards Authority (NESA) has written a limit on take-home assessment, and new rules on generative AI, into its Assessment Certification Examination (ACE) Rules for the Higher School Certificate ([register record](/policies/nsw-nesa-stage-6-take-home-assessment-ai-rules); [ACE Rule 2.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs)). The HSC limit applies from Term 4 2026, when the Class of 2027 starts its HSC courses, and the Preliminary limit from Term 1 2027 ([NESA notice](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/news/all/new-take-home-assessment-rules)).

NESA announced the limit on 31 August 2026 ([NESA notice](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/news/all/new-take-home-assessment-rules)). The Department of Education said the advice followed a request from the Minister for Education and Early Learning, Prue Car, for NESA to examine measures to protect student learning amid the rise of AI in schools ([department release](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/new-rules-for-schools-on-ai-in-take-home-hsc-assessments)). The ACE Rule pages carry change notices dated 16 September 2026 ([ACE Rule 2.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs); [ACE Rule 10.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace10/malpractice)).

## What it says

For each HSC course, schools "must not exceed one take-home assessment task, worth a maximum of 15%, except for courses with major projects or HSC practical exams that are exempt" ([ACE Rule 2.1.2](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs)). Rule 2.1.1 sets the same limit for Preliminary courses ([ACE Rule 2.1.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs)).

The rules define in-school tasks as those "completed in a classroom or formal written examination setting, or under teacher-approved monitoring conditions", including for students enrolled in distance education. Take-home tasks are any assessments that are not in-school tasks ([ACE Rule 2.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs)). Schools still decide the number, type and weighting of in-school tasks ([NESA notice](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/news/all/new-take-home-assessment-rules)).

The department puts the cap at 15% of the school-based assessment mark, or 7.5% of the total HSC mark, because school-based assessment makes up half of a student's HSC result ([department release](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/new-rules-for-schools-on-ai-in-take-home-hsc-assessments)).

Fourteen courses with a major project or HSC practical exam are exempt: Aboriginal Studies, Society and Culture, Design and Technology, Industrial Technology, Textiles and Design, Visual Arts, Dance, Drama, Music 1, Music 2, English Extension 2, Science Extension, History Extension and Music Extension ([NESA notice](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/news/all/new-take-home-assessment-rules)). HSC major works are outside the limit, but schools must still authenticate students' work ([department release](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/new-rules-for-schools-on-ai-in-take-home-hsc-assessments)).

On generative AI, schools must design HSC assessment tasks so that marks reflect a student's own knowledge, skills and understanding. Teachers must award marks "based on evidence of the student's own knowledge, skills and understanding, and not the use of generative artificial intelligence" ([ACE Rule 2.1.2](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs)).

The malpractice rule now requires schools to "not rely on software tools for the detection of generative artificial intelligence when determining whether malpractice has occured" ([ACE Rule 10.1.2](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace10/malpractice)). NESA's notice says detection software, if used at all, should be "only one input for teacher professional review, not as sole proof of AI use or malpractice" ([NESA notice](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/news/all/new-take-home-assessment-rules)). Schools and school sectors remain responsible for policies on whether generative AI is permitted in a school-based task or course ([ACE Rule 10.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace10/malpractice)).

NESA states its authority for the rules as sections 20A and 95 of the Education Act 1990 (NSW) ([ACE Rule 10.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace10/malpractice)).

## Who it affects

Schools delivering Preliminary and HSC courses must apply the limit in their school-based assessment programs ([ACE Rule 2.1](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/ace-rules/ace2/assessment-programs)). NESA asks schools to review their 2027 HSC and Preliminary assessment schedules, and to check that their malpractice policy does not rely on AI tools to detect malpractice ([NESA notice](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/news/all/new-take-home-assessment-rules)).

Students starting HSC courses in Term 4 2026 are the first cohort under the HSC limit, and students starting Year 11 in Term 1 2027 the first under the Preliminary limit ([department release](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/new-rules-for-schools-on-ai-in-take-home-hsc-assessments)). The department says the NESA Board will consider subject-specific advice and guidance for teachers, to be released from 2027 for the 2028 HSC and beyond ([department release](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/new-rules-for-schools-on-ai-in-take-home-hsc-assessments)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, this is a binding assessment rule rather than guidance. It responds to generative AI by changing where assessment happens, not by regulating the tools. That makes it unlike most government AI instruments on the register, which set conditions on how an AI system is bought, used or disclosed.

In Policai's analysis, the malpractice rule is the most transferable part. It stops a school from treating a detection score as the deciding evidence of misconduct, and leaves the finding to people following the school's own procedure. Other bodies that decide misconduct allegations about AI use, such as universities and professional examiners, face the same evidential question.

In Policai's analysis, the rules leave open how schools will verify work done under "teacher-approved monitoring conditions", and how the exemptions for major works interact with the authentication duty. The subject-specific advice promised for the 2028 HSC is the next point to watch.
