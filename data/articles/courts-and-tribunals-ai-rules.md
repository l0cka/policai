Courts and tribunals set their own rules for AI, and the rules differ. This guide covers the main instruments of the federal courts, the Fair Work Commission (FWC) and the Administrative Review Tribunal (ART), and of New South Wales, Victoria and Queensland. Names such as GPN-AI, SC GEN 25 and PNVCAT 11 are the instruments' own reference labels, and NCAT and VCAT are the NSW and Victorian civil and administrative tribunals. It also covers the South Australian and Western Australian guidelines. The earlier guide, [How AI is regulated across Australian jurisdictions](/analysis/how-ai-is-regulated-in-australia), gives courts one section. This guide compares what each instrument requires.

## What they agree on

Most instruments keep responsibility with the person who files. The Federal Court says "The presentation of false or inaccurate information to the Court is unacceptable" ([GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai), cl 4.4). Victoria's Supreme Court Practice Note makes court users responsible for their documents "whether or not a Court user has utilised AI" ([SC GEN 25](/policies/vic-supreme-court-sc-gen-25), cl 6.1).

Most require checking. The Federal Court expects the responsible person to have confirmed that cited authorities exist and support the proposition ([GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai), cl 4.5). The Queensland Supreme Court's direction requires the named practitioner to verify the accuracy and relevance of any reference to legislation, authorities or other sources in a written submission ([PD 5 of 2025](/policies/qld-supreme-court-ai-practice-direction), para 9). Several add that an AI tool cannot do the checking. From 20 October 2026, the Fair Work Commission's guidance note says "the checking must be done by a person" ([guidance note](/policies/fwc-genai-guidance-note), para 31).

Most also protect witness evidence. The Federal Court says affidavits and witness statements must reflect the person's own recollection ([GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai), cl 4.8), and the NSW Supreme Court prohibits using AI to generate their content ([SC Gen 23](/policies/nsw-supreme-court-sc-gen-23), para 10).

## Where they differ

Disclosure divides them most. The Federal Court and the Administrative Review Tribunal require disclosure when the court asks, and disclosure is mandatory for certain evidence. The Victorian Supreme Court requires no disclosure statement, but court users "must be prepared to identify the specific portions of Court documents which were produced using AI and be able to explain how the output was verified" ([SC GEN 25](/policies/vic-supreme-court-sc-gen-25), cl 7.6). The NSW Supreme Court requires an affidavit, witness statement or character reference to "contain a disclosure that Gen AI was not used" in generating its content ([SC Gen 23](/policies/nsw-supreme-court-sc-gen-23), para 13). The Queensland Supreme Court's direction on accuracy of references has no AI disclosure duty. A separate direction applies to expert reports in Supreme Court criminal proceedings commenced by indictment from 15 July 2024, other than sentences: where the expert used generative AI to help form or express an opinion, the report must name the program and how it was used, and annexe the prompts ([PD 14 of 2024](/policies/qld-supreme-court-practice-direction-14-2024-expert-evidence), para 16(l)).

South Australia's guidelines say a party filing a document "need not disclose" AI use by a particular statement, but should be able to inform a judicial officer if questioned ([SA guidelines](/policies/sa-courts-genai-guidelines), para 43). Western Australia's Supreme Court says use should be disclosed "when directed by the court or where otherwise necessary or appropriate" ([WA guidelines](/policies/wa-supreme-court-genai-guidelines), para 12).

## Confidential material

Several instruments restrict what goes into AI tools. The Family Court's direction bars entering material obtained through production, disclosure or discovery orders, material under a non-publication or suppression order, and documents produced on subpoena into any generative AI tool, unless closed-environment conditions are met ([PD-AI](/policies/family-court-ai-practice-direction), cl 5.5). VCAT "specifically prohibits" uploading material that is subject to a non-publication or suppression order, or produced on subpoena or summons ([PNVCAT 11](/policies/vic-vcat-pnvcat-11-genai), para 14). The NSW Supreme Court bars similar categories, including material produced on subpoena and information under the implied (Harman) undertaking, unless the practitioner is satisfied the information stays in a controlled environment under confidentiality restrictions ([SC Gen 23](/policies/nsw-supreme-court-sc-gen-23), para 9A). The Federal Court says that information a person may not disclose or use must not be entered into a tool contrary to those obligations ([GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai), cl 4.14 and 4.15).

## Who follows whom

Several instruments copy another. The NSW District Court and Local Court each adopt SC Gen 23 "in its current form and as it may from time to time be amended" ([District Court](/policies/nsw-district-court-genai-practice-note); [Local Court](/policies/nsw-local-court-practice-note-genai)). The Victorian County Court's practice note repeats the Supreme Court's wording almost exactly ([PNCCV 1-2026](/policies/vic-county-court-pnccv-1-2026)). The Queensland District Court's direction has the same paragraphs as the Supreme Court's ([PD 12 of 2025](/policies/qld-district-court-practice-direction-12-2025)). Other NSW and Queensland court and tribunal instruments follow one of them. The Industrial Relations Commission and the Land and Environment Court follow SC Gen 23 ([IRC](/policies/nsw-irc-practice-note-33-genai); [LEC](/policies/nsw-lec-practice-note-genai)), and the Personal Injury Commission adopts it "to the extent practicably possible", and requires written statements to contain a disclosure that AI was not used to generate them ([PIC13](/policies/nsw-pic-procedural-direction-pic13-genai)). The Land Court, the Planning and Environment Court and the Queensland Civil and Administrative Tribunal (QCAT) mirror or follow Queensland's PD 5 ([Land Court](/policies/qld-land-court-practice-direction-4-2025); [P&E Court](/policies/qld-planning-environment-court-practice-direction-7-2025); [QCAT](/policies/qld-qcat-practice-direction-10-2025)).

## People without lawyers

The Federal Court's practice note for litigants in person says not to use AI programs "to get legal help about whether you have a case you can win", but allows them to help explain a case based on real law and evidence ([GPN-LIP](/policies/federal-court-gpn-lip-ai), cl 2.8 and 2.10). The Federal Court's general note applies to litigants "whether they have legal representation or not" ([GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai), cl 3.1).

## What happens if the rules are broken

Federal Court users "should expect that there could be consequences including adverse costs orders" and issues under professional obligations ([GPN-AI](/policies/federal-court-gpn-ai), cl 5.1). The Queensland direction says a practitioner whose submissions cite non-existent material may be referred to the Legal Services Commissioner or have to show cause why a personal costs order should not be made ([PD 5 of 2025](/policies/qld-supreme-court-ai-practice-direction), para 14).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, most instruments converge on checking by the person who files, and several expect a person to be ready to explain AI use if the court asks. They diverge on disclosure and on how far AI may be used in evidence, so a lawyer who works in several courts faces different rules on those two points.

The instruments call themselves practice notes and directions, issued by a court's head of jurisdiction or by a tribunal, and guidelines in South Australia and Western Australia.
