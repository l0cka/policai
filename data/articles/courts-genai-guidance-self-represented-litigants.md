Most court rules on generative AI in the register are written for lawyers. Two are written for everyone else. Queensland's courts publish [guidelines for responsible use by non-lawyers](/policies/qld-courts-genai-guidelines-non-lawyers), dated 15 September 2025 and first issued on 13 May 2024 ([Queensland Courts](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf)). South Australia's courts published an [information sheet for self-represented litigants](/policies/sa-courts-gen-ai-self-represented-info) on 19 January 2026, alongside the [guidelines](/policies/sa-courts-genai-guidelines) that apply to practitioners and litigants ([Courts Administration Authority](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/2026/01/19/supreme-court-issues-guidelines-for-the-use-of-generative-ai)). Policai's [explainer on court AI rules](/analysis/courts-and-tribunals-ai-rules) covers the instruments aimed at practitioners.

## What it says

**Queensland.** The guidelines open by saying generative AI "is not a substitute for a qualified lawyer and cannot give you tailored legal advice", and that chatbots "have been known to provide inaccurate information on Australian law" ([guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf), Introduction). They say a chatbot may help organise facts into a clearer structure, suggest headings, and help with formatting, grammar and tone, but cannot understand the facts of a case or predict its outcome ([guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf), part 1). They ask for particular caution with affidavits and witness statements, which must reflect "the person's own knowledge and words" ([guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf), part 1).

The guidelines then set out four further points ([guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf), parts 2 to 5):

- **Confidentiality:** do not enter private, confidential, suppressed or legally privileged information into a chatbot, because what goes in could become public and breach a suppression order;
- **Accuracy:** "You are responsible for ensuring that all information you rely on or provide to the court or tribunal is accurate", chatbots can "make up fake cases, citations and quotes", and if a document with fake citations causes delay, "a costs order may be made against you";
- **Ethics:** outputs reflect gaps and biases in training data, and summarising a textbook with a chatbot could breach copyright; and
- **Security:** follow general cyber-safety practice.

Queensland's Civil and Administrative Tribunal builds the guidelines into its [Practice Direction No 10 of 2025](/policies/qld-qcat-practice-direction-10-2025). It says self-represented litigants "should read" the guidelines and "must also try to ensure the accuracy of references" in their documents and oral submissions, and that relying on non-existent cases may lead to an adjournment, doubt about the party's submissions or an adverse costs order ([QCAT](https://www.qcat.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0006/884940/qcat-practice-direction-10-of-2025-accuracy-of-references-in-submissions.pdf), paras 15 to 17).

**South Australia.** The information sheet picks two issues from the court's guidelines: restrictions on uploading documents received in a case, and verifying legal references ([information sheet](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/download/generative-ai-guidelines-and-information-sheet-for-self-represented-litigants?filename=Gen+AI+Information+Sheet+for+Self-Represented+Litigants.pdf&ind=1766530873177&refresh=694b263004da61766532656&wpdmdl=25796), p 2). It explains the Harman obligation: documents obtained through the court process, such as discovery or subpoena documents, can be used only for that proceeding ([information sheet](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/download/generative-ai-guidelines-and-information-sheet-for-self-represented-litigants?filename=Gen+AI+Information+Sheet+for+Self-Represented+Litigants.pdf&ind=1766530873177&refresh=694b263004da61766532656&wpdmdl=25796), p 2). It says those documents, suppressed material, lawyer-client documents and settlement documents should not be uploaded to a generative AI system unless the user is satisfied it is a closed system, and that uploading in breach of the Harman obligation may be found to be a contempt of court, with penalties that can include a fine or custody ([information sheet](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/download/generative-ai-guidelines-and-information-sheet-for-self-represented-litigants?filename=Gen+AI+Information+Sheet+for+Self-Represented+Litigants.pdf&ind=1766530873177&refresh=694b263004da61766532656&wpdmdl=25796), p 3).

The sheet distinguishes "open" systems, which may train on uploaded documents, from "closed" systems, which do not, gives the free version of ChatGPT as an example of an open system, and lists questions to ask a provider before uploading anything ([information sheet](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/download/generative-ai-guidelines-and-information-sheet-for-self-represented-litigants?filename=Gen+AI+Information+Sheet+for+Self-Represented+Litigants.pdf&ind=1766530873177&refresh=694b263004da61766532656&wpdmdl=25796), p 4). On accuracy, it says "If you give the court information from Gen AI that turns out to be wrong, you are responsible for that mistake", gives worked examples of better and worse prompts, and points to free resources for checking cases and legislation ([information sheet](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/download/generative-ai-guidelines-and-information-sheet-for-self-represented-litigants?filename=Gen+AI+Information+Sheet+for+Self-Represented+Litigants.pdf&ind=1766530873177&refresh=694b263004da61766532656&wpdmdl=25796), pp 4 to 6). It adds: "The only way you can receive legal advice is to instruct a qualified lawyer." ([information sheet](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/download/generative-ai-guidelines-and-information-sheet-for-self-represented-litigants?filename=Gen+AI+Information+Sheet+for+Self-Represented+Litigants.pdf&ind=1766530873177&refresh=694b263004da61766532656&wpdmdl=25796), p 6).

## Who it affects

Queensland's guidelines apply to civil and criminal proceedings across the state's courts and tribunals, from the Supreme Court to the Magistrates Courts, the Land Court, the Childrens Court, the industrial jurisdictions and QCAT ([guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf), Introduction). They are addressed to non-lawyers who represent themselves or others, which the guidelines define to include self-represented litigants, McKenzie friends, lay advocates and employment advocates ([guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/867502/artificial-intelligence-guidelines-for-non-lawyers.pdf), Appendix A).

South Australia's guidelines were adopted from 1 January 2026 and apply in the Supreme, District, Magistrates, Youth and Environment, Resources and Development Courts and the Court of Disputed Returns; the court said they "will shortly be given force under Rules of the Court" ([Courts Administration Authority](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/2026/01/19/supreme-court-issues-guidelines-for-the-use-of-generative-ai)). The information sheet is the court's "additional guidance for self-represented litigants" ([Courts Administration Authority](https://www.courts.sa.gov.au/2026/01/19/supreme-court-issues-guidelines-for-the-use-of-generative-ai)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the two documents answer different questions. Queensland's is a literacy guide: it explains how chatbots work and why they get the law wrong, then puts responsibility for accuracy on the user. South Australia's starts from what a self-represented person might do with a chatbot that a lawyer would know not to, and names the obligation that would be breached. A lawyer meets the Harman obligation in practice; a litigant in person may first meet it as a contempt finding.

In Policai's analysis, the consequences differ in weight. Queensland's guidelines and QCAT's direction point to costs and adjournments. South Australia's sheet points to contempt, which reaches further. Both rest on the same premise as the practitioner rules: whoever files the document answers for it, whether or not a tool wrote it.

In Policai's analysis, South Australia's open and closed distinction is practical but dated by design. It relies on a named product and on wording shown in that product's interface, both of which can change without the court's sheet changing. Queensland avoids that by telling users not to enter sensitive material at all. Either way, the guidance assumes a self-represented person can tell which kind of tool they are using, which is the hardest part for the people it is written for.
