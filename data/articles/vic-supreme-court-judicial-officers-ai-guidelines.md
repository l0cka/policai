The Supreme Court of Victoria has published [Guidelines: The Use of Artificial Intelligence by Judicial Officers](/policies/vic-supreme-court-ai-guidelines-judicial-officers), dated 14 May 2026, which set the limits on how the Court's own judicial officers may use AI ([Guidelines](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf), cl 1.1). The Court published them on [its guidelines page](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/forms-fees-and-services/forms-templates-and-guidelines/guidelines-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence-by-judicial-officers). They say they were informed by the eight principles the Victorian Law Reform Commission formulated in its October 2025 report on AI in Victoria's courts and tribunals ([Guidelines](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf), cl 1.2 and note 1).

## What it says

The core rule is one sentence: "Judicial Officers must not use GenAI for judicial decision-making" ([Guidelines](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf), cl 3.1). Judicial officers consider each matter, exercise their own judgment to reach a decision and, where appropriate, give reasons ([cl 3.2](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)).

That rule does not prevent supportive uses of AI by judicial officers or Court staff ([cl 3.3](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)). The permitted uses listed are organising and locating case materials, producing summaries and chronologies from them, aiding legal research and proof-reading ([cl 3.4](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)). These uses "are not a substitute for reading and/or listening to evidence and submissions, or fact finding" ([cl 3.4](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)).

For case-related matters, judicial officers and Court staff must use only AI tools the Court has approved, and approval turns on protecting confidentiality and reducing other risks ([cl 3.5](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)). The Guidelines contrast "public AI", openly accessible tools trained on broad datasets, with "closed AI", where controls protect information from being made public or used to train the tool ([cl 2.1](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)). Judicial officers should give their staff clear directions on appropriate AI use and on the level of disclosure they require ([cl 3.6](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)). The Court intends to review the Guidelines as technology and its experience change ([cl 3.7](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)).

## Who it affects

The Guidelines apply to judicial officers, defined as a Judge, Associate Judge or Judicial Registrar of the Court, including reserve judges and reserve associate judges ([cl 2.1](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)), and in part to Court staff ([cl 3.3 and 3.5](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2026-05/Use%20of%20AI%20by%20Judicial%20Officers%20Guidelines.pdf)). Lawyers and litigants are governed separately by the Court's practice note on AI use by court users, [SC GEN 25](/policies/vic-supreme-court-sc-gen-25), covered in Policai's [guide to court and tribunal AI rules](/analysis/courts-and-tribunals-ai-rules).

The County Court of Victoria's [guidelines for its judicial officers](/policies/vic-county-court-judicial-ai-guidelines), created on 5 June 2026 and authorised by Chief Judge Chambers, contain the same operative rules ([County Court guidelines](https://www.countycourt.vic.gov.au/files/documents/2026-06/guidelines-use-ai-judicial-officers.docx)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the Victorian approach draws its line at the decision rather than at the task. New South Wales goes further on drafting: its guidelines say judges should not use generative AI to formulate reasons or assess evidence, and should not use it to edit or proof draft judgments ([NSW guidelines](https://supremecourt.nsw.gov.au/content/dam/dcj/ctsd/supreme-court/documents/About-the-Court/policies/Guidelines_Gen_AI.pdf), paras 4 and 5). Victoria lists proof-reading as a permitted supportive use, but only through Court-approved tools for case-related matters. Queensland's guidelines say AI tools should not be used for decision-making or to develop or prepare reasons ([Queensland guidelines](https://www.courts.qld.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0004/890428/the-use-of-generative-ai-guidelines-for-judicial-officers.pdf), para 25).

The Guidelines say nothing about whether parties are told when a judicial officer or their staff used AI for supportive work. Disclosure runs only from staff to the judicial officer, at the level the judicial officer sets. The Guidelines also do not list which tools the Court has approved, so how much supportive use is possible in practice is not public.
