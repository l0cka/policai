The committee chair, Jo Briskey, presented the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence's report, "Artificial intelligence: The Australian way. In our interest. On our terms.", in the House of Representatives on 24 November 2026, and it was made a Parliamentary Paper ([House Votes and Proceedings No 96](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/search/display/display.w3p;query=Id%3A%22chamber%2Fvotes%2Ff93b30fe-0ef3-407e-a54a-2974f74613a8%2F0008%22)). The committee was appointed on 20 August 2026 and had to report by 30 November 2026; on 12 October it sent the Prime Minister a letter of preliminary findings and recommendations, reproduced at Appendix 1 ([report, chapter 1](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Chapter_1_-_Introduction), paras 1.1 to 1.2). The report makes 46 recommendations to the Australian Government, and carries additional comments from Coalition members, Labor members and Kate Chaney MP, and a dissenting report from Senator David Pocock ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report)).

## What it says

The committee calls a new Parliamentary Joint Standing Committee on AI "the key recommendation of this inquiry report" ([report, chapter 1](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Chapter_1_-_Introduction), para 1.12). It says the AI Safety Institute, the AI Safety Regulator, the Office of AI and the AI Incident Review Board could report to that committee on serious events and incidents, and that the committee would publish details of incidents involving unauthorised actions of AI agents ([report, front material](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Artificial_intelligence__the_Australian_way), section 1.1).

The safeguards recommendations ask the government to ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report), recommendations 2 to 12):

- "urgently consider and deliver on the need to provide additional funding" to the AI Safety Institute (recommendation 2);
- establish "a proportionate, risk-based framework setting standards for the development and deployment of AI systems", with requirements for model evaluation, safety testing, cybersecurity, transparency, accountability, human oversight and ongoing monitoring, and "a robust compliance and penalties mechanism for breaches" (recommendation 3);
- require frontier labs to monitor and report incidents to the government "in a timely manner", with "clear reporting deadlines" (recommendation 4), and mandate independent evaluation and auditing for frontier labs that operate in Australia (recommendation 5);
- expand the "Limited Use Protections" in the Intelligence Services Act 2001 and the Cyber Security Act 2024, so that information given to government about potential unauthorised activity cannot be used by regulators to apply penalties (recommendation 6);
- mandate, as part of any arrangement for frontier lab training in Australia, early access to new models for agencies such as the Australian Signals Directorate and the AI Safety Institute (recommendation 7); and
- review the measures 12 months after any of them commences (recommendation 12).

The committee calls the recommendation 3 standards "AI Safety Standards" and says they should address the risks of advanced and frontier AI systems "while avoiding unnecessary regulatory burdens that would inhibit innovation or investment" ([report, front material](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Artificial_intelligence__the_Australian_way), section 1.3).

On harms, it recommends that the Joint Standing Committee on Electoral Matters examine banning deepfakes in political advertising, that the government consider mandating machine-readable labels on AI-generated and materially AI-altered photos, videos and voice recordings, and that it consider mechanisms giving Australians "the right to control how their face, voice and likeness are used" ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report), recommendations 17 to 19). It also asks for a Commonwealth framework for AI in government decision making, and for legal frameworks able to hold humans or companies accountable when AI agents do tasks they would otherwise be responsible for ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report), recommendations 20 and 22).

On infrastructure, recommendation 25 is that the government "implements a licensing framework to enforce the AI Infrastructure Standards" ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report)). The committee says it wants nationally consistent AI Infrastructure Standards set with the states and territories, and that, "consistent with the government's proposed National Standards for Data Centres", AI infrastructure should bring its own power, use water responsibly and be located in appropriate areas ([report, front material](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Artificial_intelligence__the_Australian_way), section 3). It also asks the government to consider reserving some compute capacity for the national interest as a condition of permitting significant AI infrastructure projects ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report), recommendation 28).

On copyright, recommendations 31 to 38 ask the government to strengthen copyright protections, including "a mechanism to make it easier and fairer for Australian rights holders and creators to be paid by AI companies training frontier and other large language models in Australia", with "strong penalties for failure to comply with the law", and to require AI companies training models in Australia to be "transparent and accountable for the data they use" ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report), recommendations 31 and 37). Recommendation 36 asks for settings that give frontier AI companies "the certainty necessary to train their AI models in Australia" ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report)).

The members did not agree on energy. The committee says it "was unable to reach a consensus position" on how far the government should specify energy sources for AI infrastructure ([report, front material](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Artificial_intelligence__the_Australian_way), section 3.1). Coalition members support considering gas, high-efficiency, low-emissions coal and nuclear power alongside renewables ([Coalition additional comments](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Additional_comments_from_Coalition_members), para 1.8). Labor members want AI developers to "bring their own, new, renewable generation", backed up by batteries, pumped hydro or gas ([Labor additional comments](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Additional_comments_from_Labor_members), para 1.7). Kate Chaney says she "strongly support[s]" the report, and that a recommendation on large AI data centres bringing their own additional renewable energy was left out because "multipartisan agreement could not be reached" ([Chaney additional comments](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Additional_comments_from_Ms_Kate_Chaney_MP), paras 1.2 and 1.25).

Senator Pocock says he supports many of the recommendations but dissents for three reasons: a more cautious approach, protecting copyright, and ensuring data centres do not raise power prices and emissions ([dissenting report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Dissenting_report_from_Senator_David_Pocock), para 1.3). His own recommendations include an overarching National AI Safety Act, no changes to copyright law for the next three years pending litigation overseas, independent pre-deployment testing by companies training models in Australia, with new models approved for release by the AI Safety Institute, and a pause on data centre approvals until national rules are enacted ([dissenting report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Dissenting_report_from_Senator_David_Pocock), paras 1.243, 1.287, 1.292 and 1.300). He also asks the government to legislate his [My Face, My Rights Bill](/policies/federal-online-safety-other-legislation-amendment-my-face-my-rights-bill-2025) ([dissenting report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Dissenting_report_from_Senator_David_Pocock), para 1.317).

## Who it affects

Nothing in the report binds anyone yet. It is a set of recommendations to the government, presented to the House and referred to the Federation Chamber for debate ([House Votes and Proceedings No 96](https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/search/display/display.w3p;query=Id%3A%22chamber%2Fvotes%2Ff93b30fe-0ef3-407e-a54a-2974f74613a8%2F0008%22)). Its main targets are frontier AI labs that operate or train models in Australia (recommendations 4, 5 and 7), developers of AI infrastructure and data centres (recommendations 25 and 28), and AI companies training models in Australia, in their dealings with rights holders and creators (recommendations 31 to 38) ([report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report)).

The report lists the government measures it builds on: the [National AI Plan](/policies/national-ai-plan-2025) of December 2025, the AI Safety Institute established in early 2026, the [Expectations of data centres and AI infrastructure developers](/policies/expectations-data-centres-ai-infrastructure-2026) published in March 2026, the Office of AI established in July 2026, and a set of Australian Standards for AI "expected to be introduced to Parliament in early 2027" ([report, chapter 1](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Chapter_1_-_Introduction), paras 1.19 to 1.22).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, much of the safeguards list was already answered in outline by the government's 10 November announcement, covered in Policai's [note on the Rapid Review](/analysis/pmc-rapid-review-recommendations-2026). That release lists six obligations for frontier AI companies, including independent evaluation of their models and rapid mandatory incident notification, to be enforced by a new regulator with significant penalties, and says the protections "are aligned with interim report recommendations from the Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence" ([Prime Minister](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/new-australian-ai-safety-and-security-protections)). Recommendations 4 and 5 restate that ground. The parts the release does not mention are where the final report adds something: early model access for ASD and the AI Safety Institute, protecting incident information from use in penalties, more money for the Institute, a standing committee to oversee all of it, and a review after 12 months.

In Policai's analysis, recommendation 3 goes wider than the frontier protections. It asks for standards for "the development and deployment of AI systems" generally, not only for frontier labs, with penalties for breaches. That is closer to the economy-wide mandatory guardrails the government consulted on in 2024 and set aside in the National AI Plan ([record](/policies/safe-and-responsible-ai-australia-proposals-paper)). Senator Pocock calls the Prime Minister's recent six point plan "a welcome pivot away from a voluntary approach and return to the mandatory guardrails previously espoused" ([dissenting report](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Report/Dissenting_report_from_Senator_David_Pocock), para 1.1), but the 10 November obligations apply to frontier AI companies. How far the Australian AI Standards legislation reaches beyond frontier companies is the question to watch when it is introduced.

In Policai's analysis, the copyright recommendations pull two ways. Most of recommendations 31 to 38 aim to protect and pay rights holders and creators, with penalties and training-data transparency, while recommendation 36 asks for the certainty frontier companies say they need to train here. The recommendations do not say which gives way if they conflict, and Senator Pocock's dissent would change nothing for three years. The infrastructure licensing framework in recommendation 25 has a similar gap: it does not say who would issue the licences, and the energy terms they would enforce are exactly where the members could not agree.
