The Digital Transformation Agency (DTA) published version 2.1 of the [Policy for the Responsible Use of AI in Government](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2) on 21 October 2026. The DTA describes the release as "minor updates" to version 2.0, which came into effect on 15 December 2025 ([DTA policy page](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy)). The live text includes Appendix D, a classification system for AI use that agencies must apply when they record, assess and publicly report their AI use cases ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)).

## What it says

The policy applies to all non-corporate Commonwealth entities, with specified exceptions ([register record](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2)). It requires accountable officials, transparency statements, a strategic approach to AI adoption, operational controls for responsible AI use, AI use case accountability and registers, staff training, and AI use case impact assessments ([register record](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2); [version 2.1 PDF](https://www.digital.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2026-10/Policy%20for%20the%20responsible%20use%20of%20AI%20in%20government%202_1_1.pdf)).

Appendix D classifies AI use into seven usage patterns: decision-making and administrative action, analytics, strategic support, workplace productivity, workflow automation, image processing, and coding ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)). It also sorts use into six domains: service delivery; compliance and fraud detection; law enforcement, intelligence and security; policy and legal; scientific; and corporate and enabling ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)).

The first pattern is the one with the most direct effect on the public. Appendix D defines decision-making and administrative action as use of AI "to make, or substantially and directly inform, a decision or administrative action that could affect the rights or interests of an individual or entity", and adds that "this includes refusing or failing to take such an action" ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)). Its examples go beyond final decisions to "risk scoring, prioritisation recommendations, case triage, benefit assessment support and recommended enforcement actions for human decision makers" ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)).

Two other patterns are drawn more narrowly. Workflow automation "executes or coordinates predefined processes, tasks or actions with limited discretion", and workplace productivity supports routine work "without materially changing business processes or administrative outcomes" ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)). Image processing lists "facial recognition for border control" among its examples ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)).

Agencies must apply the classification in three places: in their use case register, as the [Standard for accountability](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/accountability) requires; when they assess a use case with the AI impact assessment tool; and in their AI transparency statement, as the [Standard for AI transparency statements](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/standard-ai-transparency-statements) requires ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)). The register links both standards at version 2.1 ([register record](/policies/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-v2)).

## Who it affects

The policy is mandatory for non-corporate Commonwealth entities and encouraged for corporate Commonwealth entities; the Defence portfolio and the National Intelligence Community are listed as exceptions ([version 2.1 PDF](https://www.digital.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2026-10/Policy%20for%20the%20responsible%20use%20of%20AI%20in%20government%202_1_1.pdf)). Within those agencies, the classification falls to the officials who keep the internal use case register, run impact assessments and publish the transparency statement ([Appendix D](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/policy-responsible-use-ai-government-version-21-appendix-d-classification-system-ai-use)).

Under the policy, agencies must assess existing AI use cases by 30 April 2027, and high-risk use cases must be reported to the agency's accountable official and to the DTA before deployment ([version 2.1 PDF](https://www.digital.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/2026-10/Policy%20for%20the%20responsible%20use%20of%20AI%20in%20government%202_1_1.pdf)). People and businesses dealing with Commonwealth agencies are affected indirectly, through what agencies disclose about their AI use in transparency statements ([Standard for AI transparency statements](https://www.digital.gov.au/ai/ai-in-government-policy/standard-ai-transparency-statements)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the decision-making pattern is the part to watch. By counting triage, risk scoring and recommendations to human decision makers as decision-making, Appendix D puts assistive tools in the same reporting category as systems that decide outright. An agency cannot keep a triage model out of that category simply because a person signs off the final decision.

In Policai's analysis, the classification also gives a common vocabulary across agencies. Because the same patterns and domains appear in registers, impact assessments and transparency statements, readers should in time be able to compare how different agencies describe similar uses. Whether that happens depends on how consistently agencies apply the categories, which the published statements will show.

In Policai's analysis, the definition sits close to the Privacy Act transparency test that applies from 10 December 2026. [APP 1.7](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance) reaches computer programs that make a decision, or do a thing substantially and directly related to making one, that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual's rights or interests ([register record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)). APP 1.7 applies to APP entities, and only where personal information about the individual is used in the program's operation ([register record](/policies/oaic-app-1-7-adm-transparency-guidance)). The two tests are worded differently and serve different purposes, so one does not settle the other, but a use an agency classifies as decision-making under Appendix D may also raise the APP 1.7 question where personal information is involved.
