The Australian Signals Directorate (ASD) released [Frontier AI cyber threat considerations for boards of directors](/policies/new-ai-frontier-cyber-threat-guidance-for-boards-of-director) in partnership with the Australian Institute of Company Directors (AICD) ([ASD news](https://www.cyber.gov.au/news/new-ai-frontier-cyber-threat-guidance-for-boards-of-directors)). The downloadable publication is dated August 2026 ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)).

## What it says

The guidance says frontier AI models can identify vulnerabilities and rapidly weaponise them, chain several low-severity vulnerabilities into high-impact compromises, and carry out malicious cyber activity with little to no human oversight ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)). It says frontier AI "has the potential to significantly reduce vulnerability discovery and exploitation timelines from days to hours" while lowering the skill needed by malicious actors, and that this "may rapidly invalidate organisations' current risk tolerance" ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)).

It also asks boards to look beyond security operations, at cyber supply chain risk from relying on particular AI vendors and the foreign ownership, control or influence risks that reliance may pose ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)).

The core of the guidance is a set of threshold questions for boards to discuss with management. They include ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)):

- what assumptions underpin current risk assessments, and how frontier AI threats might invalidate them;
- whether minor weaknesses in systems and the cyber supply chain could be combined into a major incident;
- whether the organisation relies on vendors without sufficient oversight, including of their foreign ownership, control or influence;
- what legacy technology risks are being carried, and whether weaknesses are being left because of perceived low exposure or severity; and
- whether the organisation could still detect and respond "if attacks moved from taking days to hours", and whether incident response and business continuity plans have been updated and tested for frontier AI threats.

It then sets priorities for boards to "oversee and challenge management" on ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)):

- immediate: secure attack surfaces and reduce software vulnerabilities;
- short term: replace legacy systems, reinforce identity, credential and access management, restrict unnecessary privileges (including for AI agents), and prepare for incidents;
- medium term: adopt AI for cyber defence that is "secure, controllable, human-supervised"; and
- longer term: modernise systems using Secure by Design and Secure by Default principles.

Each priority comes with practical actions, such as phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication, scanning codebases and collaboration platforms for exposed secrets, and regularly exercising incident response and recovery plans. The guidance says boards should expect "regular reporting and assurance" on these ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)).

## Who it affects

The publication is written for board directors and senior leaders ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors)). It is guidance, framed as what boards "should" discuss and oversee, not a legal instrument ([ASD publication](https://www.cyber.gov.au/publication/frontier-ai-cyber-threat-considerations-for-boards-of-directors); [record](/policies/new-ai-frontier-cyber-threat-guidance-for-boards-of-director)).

For banks, insurers, superannuation trustees and other regulated financial entities, it sits beside APRA and ASIC's joint paper [Resilience at Frontier AI Speed](/policies/asic-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-2026), which sets expectations and preparedness measures for frontier-AI-related cyber, technology and operational risks ([record](/policies/asic-apra-resilience-at-frontier-ai-speed-2026)).

## Analysis

In Policai's analysis, the guidance is the board-level counterpart to ASD's operational advice, such as its September alert on [AI misalignment](/policies/asd-risks-ai-misalignment-2026) ([record](/policies/asd-risks-ai-misalignment-2026)). Most of its controls are ordinary cyber hygiene. What it adds is a timing claim, that exploitation may move from days to hours, and a request that boards test whether their organisation's risk tolerance still holds under that claim.

In Policai's analysis, the supply chain point is the part most likely to be new to boards. Treating dependence on an AI vendor, and that vendor's ownership and control, as a board-level cyber risk goes beyond the patching and access controls that make up most of the document.

In Policai's analysis, the guidance has gained relevance since its release. On 10 November 2026 the government accepted all 18 recommendations of the Rapid Review into the AI-driven cyber incident, which include "strengthening resilience and response to AI incidents, including improved information sharing and a renewed focus on cyber security" ([Prime Minister's media release](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/strengthening-australia-against-ai-driven-cyber-incidents)). That review is directed at government arrangements. For private-sector boards, this guidance is the register's most direct statement of what ASD expects directors to ask.
